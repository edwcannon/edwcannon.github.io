Good Talk: Kairotic Affordance and the Ethics of Disclosure

On timing, proportion, relationship, and the possibility of leaving another person somewhere to stand.

By Ed W. Cannon

A few nights ago, Ed sent his father a text.

He had been considering the possibility of moving from Las Vegas to Los Angeles. The move was not decided. It might not happen at all. But his parents were planning to stop in Las Vegas on their way to a vacation in October, in part because they wanted to see him. If he left before then, their plans might no longer make sense.

For some time, he had felt that he ought to tell them.

The difficulty was that he did not yet know what there was to tell.

“I’m moving to Los Angeles” would have been false. “I’m thinking about moving to Los Angeles” was true, but could communicate too little about the practical possibility while suggesting too much about its certainty. He could explain all of his reasons—the books, the screenplay, promotional possibilities, work, the appeal of a different environment—but doing so might make a tentative possibility sound like a declaration requiring evaluation.

And there was another consideration particular to the relationship.

His father loves him and worries about him. Ed also knows that his father responds especially well to practical information. A dramatic account of a possible relocation could therefore produce a conversation about the wisdom of the move before there was yet a move to discuss.

The relevant question at that moment was smaller:

If I am no longer here when you arrive, would you be able to change your plans, and would you still want to?

For a while, Ed said nothing.

Then, without sitting down to compose the perfect message or consciously resolving the problem, he picked up his phone and wrote:

if i dont end up staying in Vegas by October 7th would u be able to change ur plans / would u want to still? i may move to Los Angeles for book and screenplay promotion options

And there it was.

Nothing important about the external situation had suddenly changed.

He still did not know whether he would move. His parents’ plans were the same. Los Angeles remained a possibility rather than a decision.

What had changed was that the thing he wanted to say had finally found a form in which it could truthfully be said.

That distinction is easy to miss.

We often imagine communication as though the central ethical question were simply whether a statement is true. If something is true, say it. If it is not true, do not.

This is necessary as far as it goes, but human speech rarely presents us with problems so simple. Much of what matters between people occurs while facts remain incomplete, intentions remain provisional, and consequences have begun to emerge before certainty has.

The harder questions are therefore not merely:

Is this true?

They are also:

Is this the part of the truth that matters now?

How much of it belongs to the other person?

What does the other person need in order to act fairly?

What will the way I say it imply beyond what I actually know?

And is this the moment in which speaking opens something—or closes something prematurely?

These are questions of timing, proportion, relationship, and form.

They are questions of Kairos.

The Opportunity to Speak

I use the term Kairotic Affordance to describe an opportunity made possible by the particular structure of a moment.

An affordance is not fate.

The existence of an opening does not determine what someone will do with it, nor does it guarantee that the opening will remain. It is simply a real possibility that becomes available under particular circumstances.

We usually notice these possibilities in action.

Someone happens to meet a person who can help with a project. A conversation unexpectedly becomes intimate. An acquaintance becomes a friendship. A door opens professionally. Someone finally asks the question another person has been waiting to answer.

But speech is itself an action.

And language has affordances of its own.

There are moments in which something can be said that could not have been said properly before—not necessarily because the information was unavailable, but because its appropriate form had not yet become clear.

This suggests a particular kind of Kairotic Affordance:

an affordance for disclosure.

Ed already possessed most of the relevant facts before sending the message.

He knew Los Angeles was a possibility.

He knew the approximate timing.

He knew his parents had plans involving him.

He knew that informing them too late could interfere with their ability to decide for themselves.

He also knew that presenting the move too confidently could create unnecessary concern.

What he did not yet possess was the sentence capable of preserving all of those truths at once.

The eventual message did something deceptively sophisticated.

Its conditional opening—if I don’t end up staying in Vegas—preserved uncertainty rather than disguising it.

Its date gave that uncertainty practical boundaries.

Its question—would you be able to change your plans / would you want to still?—centered his parents’ agency rather than demanding that they immediately evaluate his possible move.

Its brief explanation—book and screenplay promotion options—gave enough reason for the possibility to be intelligible without turning one logistical message into a defense of his entire future.

He did not say everything.

He said enough.

And that difference may be one of the most useful distinctions available to us in ordinary communication.

The Fantasy of Complete Explanation

There is a familiar impulse, particularly when we care very much about being understood, to explain as much as possible and then allow the other person to sort through it.

The instinct often comes from somewhere sincere.

If misunderstanding is the danger, perhaps more information appears to be the cure.

So we provide the context, the history, the qualifications, the motives, the fears, the possible outcomes, the reasons behind those outcomes, and perhaps even our predictions about how the listener might respond.

We think:

If I can just explain the whole thing, then they will understand.

Sometimes they will.

But abundance can also become its own form of obscurity.

The listener receives so many possible points of response that the central matter becomes difficult to locate.

Tentative thoughts begin to resemble commitments simply because they have been described at length.

Explanations offered in anticipation of resistance can create the emotional shape of an argument before anybody has objected.

And perhaps most importantly, the other person can lose their place in the conversation because we have already narrated so much of what the interaction might become.

There is a strange way in which excessive explanation can make a conversation less conversational.

One person has externalized the entire architecture of the problem and then handed it across the table.

Here is everything. Please sort me out.

But a relationship is not merely the transfer of one complete interior world into another.

Good talk requires somewhere for the other person to stand.

That means communication cannot always be measured by completeness.

Sometimes its task is much humbler:

to say enough for the next honest interaction to become possible.

Ed’s text did not decide whether he would move to Los Angeles.

It did not decide how his parents would feel if he did.

It did not decide whether they would change their plans, meet somewhere else, or what any of those possibilities would ultimately mean for the relationship.

It simply gave his father something real to answer.

And because it did not attempt to determine everything that followed, another moment was allowed to arrive.

What Is Speech For?

This raises a larger question.

What makes talk good?

The word telos is useful here.

For Aristotle, to ask about the telos of something is, roughly, to ask after its end: what is it for, and what would it mean for it to perform that function well?

The problem becomes immediately interesting when we apply the question to speech.

What is speech for?

“Communicating information” is too narrow.

We use words to warn, flirt, comfort, promise, confess, invite, refuse, instruct, joke, challenge, apologize, provoke, negotiate, mourn, entertain, inquire, and sometimes simply to enjoy the presence of another person.

The telos of a warning is not identical to the telos of a joke.

A confession does not succeed by the same criteria as small talk.

A marriage proposal, a legal argument, an apology, a goodbye, and a ridiculous joke between friends are all acts of speech, but they are trying to accomplish profoundly different things.

There may therefore be no single quantity of speech that is always right.

Maximum disclosure cannot be the universal standard because sometimes tact is virtuous.

Restraint cannot be the universal standard because sometimes silence is cowardice.

Speed cannot be the universal standard because some thoughts need time.

Waiting cannot be the universal standard because some opportunities disappear almost as quickly as they arrive.

The appropriate end of speech is partly given by the occasion.

In that sense, Kairos helps specify telos locally.

The moment asks something of us.

Not in the sense that circumstances dictate one inevitable sentence, but in the sense that a situation changes what different kinds of speech can accomplish.

A warning given tomorrow may no longer warn.

A confession given too early may burden someone with a relationship that does not yet exist.

An apology given too late may still matter, but it no longer occupies the same moral space.

A joke can save an awkward moment or ruin a funeral.

The same words can be courageous in one circumstance and grotesquely mistimed in another.

So good speech cannot be understood apart from occasion.

Truth matters.

But truth must meet timing.

Sincerity matters.

But sincerity must meet proportion.

Clarity matters.

But clarity must meet relationship.

And all of these must somehow meet another human being.

Expression and Communication

There is another distinction worth making.

Expression asks:

What do I need to get out?

Communication asks:

What am I saying to this person, here?

Expression is not bad. Human beings need it.

Art may sometimes depend upon its excesses.

Journals exist partly because not every thought needs to be negotiated socially.

A person may need to say everything somewhere before discovering what actually belongs in the conversation.

But communication introduces another consciousness into the act.

The listener is not a container into which I deposit myself.

They have their own knowledge, vulnerabilities, desires, interpretations, and decisions to make.

That means good communication requires some attention to what our speech makes possible for them.

This is not manipulation.

In fact, manipulation usually attempts to reduce the other person’s possibilities.

It tries to make one response seem inevitable.

Good speech should often do the opposite.

It should preserve enough shared reality for the other person to genuinely answer.

We might therefore say:

Good speech aims without controlling.

It has a purpose without dictating the conclusion.

Ed wanted his father to understand that the October plans might need to change.

But he did not need his father to approve of Los Angeles in order to communicate that.

He did not need to win the argument for a move that had not yet happened.

He did not need to determine how his father should feel.

He needed to create a truthful enough shared situation for his father to exercise his own judgment.

That is one possible telos of good dialogue:

not to finish the relationship in a single act of speech, but to make another honest act possible.

Timely Uncertainty

We tend to admire certainty in communication.

We tell people to “be clear.”

Often that is excellent advice.

But clarity and certainty are not identical.

Sometimes the clearest possible statement is:

I do not know yet.

The ethical question then becomes whether uncertainty can itself be communicated with sufficient precision.

Ed’s text accomplished what we might call timely uncertainty.

He did not wait until uncertainty disappeared.

Had he done so, his parents might have lost meaningful time to alter their travel plans.

Nor did he transform uncertainty into false confidence simply because speaking confidently is easier to interpret.

He communicated the uncertainty at the point when the uncertainty itself had become relevant to another person’s agency.

That gives us a useful principle:

Uncertainty belongs to another person when its consequences begin to meaningfully affect their choices.

Not every possibility must immediately be announced.

We entertain hundreds of possibilities about our work, relationships, futures, and identities. Most die before becoming consequential.

But there is a threshold at which private uncertainty becomes relationally relevant.

That threshold will differ from case to case.

If I am vaguely considering taking another job, my coworker may not need to know.

If I am the only person scheduled to open the restaurant tomorrow and I am seriously considering leaving town tonight, the situation changes.

If I am contemplating ending a relationship during one difficult afternoon, immediate disclosure may cause more harm than clarity.

If I have been preparing to leave for three months while allowing the other person to make major life decisions under the assumption that I am staying, silence begins to take on a different ethical character.

There is no formula capable of resolving every case.

But there is a question:

When does what I am carrying begin to alter what another person has reasonable cause to do?

That is often when uncertainty itself becomes something worth speaking.

The Listener’s Right to Agency

This helps us understand why “how much should I disclose?” cannot be answered simply by appealing to personal privacy or total transparency.

The better question may be:

What does the other person presently need in order to retain appropriate agency?

That word—appropriate—matters.

No one is entitled to the contents of another person’s mind simply because they are curious.

A stranger does not acquire a right to our full biography because they have asked a personal question.

A new friend does not automatically deserve every history that might eventually matter to a deeper friendship.

Parents do not acquire complete jurisdiction over an adult child’s choices merely because those choices worry them.

Likewise, privacy cannot become an excuse for withholding information upon which another person is reasonably relying.

Good disclosure is neither total exposure nor absolute self-sovereignty.

It is relationally proportionate.

The question is not:

How much information can I possibly give?

Nor is it:

How little can I get away with saying?

It is:

What truth does this other person need in order to stand honestly inside the situation we currently share?

In Ed’s case, his parents did not presently need a manifesto defending Los Angeles.

They needed to know that there was a meaningful possibility he would not be in Las Vegas when they arrived.

That was enough to restore their agency over their own plans.

Leaving Somewhere to Stand

There is a conversational virtue hidden in all of this that may be even simpler.

Leave the other person somewhere to stand.

When we overexplain, we sometimes unknowingly speak on both sides of the conversation.

We state what happened.

Then why we did it.

Then why someone might misunderstand it.

Then why that misunderstanding would be unfair.

Then what we imagine they are probably feeling.

Then why they should not feel that.

Then what we expect them to say.

Then why that response would also be mistaken.

By the time we finish, the listener has technically been given an enormous amount of information and almost no room to participate.

Good talk is not necessarily short.

Sometimes a person needs twenty minutes to say something honestly.

But even long speech can remain open.

The issue is whether the speech treats the other person as somebody from whom something genuinely new might come.

Conversation requires epistemic space.

I cannot completely know your answer before you give it.

If I believe I do, I am no longer quite talking with you.

I am performing a model of you in your presence.

That is one reason restraint can sometimes be an act of respect.

Not because the speaker lacks more to say.

Because the listener still has something to contribute.

The Danger of Making Caution a Religion

But there is an obvious danger in everything I have written so far.

A philosophy of timing can easily become a philosophy of hesitation.

That would be a mistake.

The lesson is not:

Wait until you have perfectly calculated the consequences of every sentence.

No one could live like that.

And frankly, nobody would want to have dinner with us.

Some of the best speech is improvisational.

Someone says something funny and the reply is already there.

A friend unexpectedly admits that they are struggling and the words I’m here arrive without philosophical consultation.

Someone crosses a boundary and no needs very little ornament.

A stranger opens the door to conversation.

Someone flirts.

Someone challenges us.

Someone asks exactly the question we have secretly wanted somebody to ask.

There are moments when deliberation would destroy the moment it is attempting to perfect.

The discipline therefore cannot simply be waiting.

It is something subtler:

learning to distinguish the urge to speak from the availability of something worth saying.

Sometimes they arrive together.

Then speak.

Sometimes they do not.

Then waiting may itself be intelligent.

This distinction also preserves something essential to human charm.

We should not become so frightened of error that every social interaction feels like filing a legal brief.

A little risk is part of conversation.

A joke can fail.

An invitation can be declined.

A flirtation can be unanswered.

A vulnerable admission can be met awkwardly.

The purpose of Kairotic awareness is not to eliminate risk.

It is to become more sensitive to the possibilities that risk serves.

Sometimes good talk requires jumping.

Sometimes it requires holding your tongue.

Wisdom lies partly in learning the difference.

A Small Practice

None of this provides a perfect decision procedure.

But it gives us a handful of questions worth carrying.

Before speaking, when time permits, I might ask:

What do I actually know?

Not what do I fear, suspect, predict, or hope—but what can I honestly say is presently true?

Why does this person need to know?

Is there some choice, expectation, understanding, or relationship that the information meaningfully affects?

How much is enough?

What must be communicated now, and what can remain unresolved until reality resolves more of it for us?

What can this moment carry?

Is this the right setting, tone, and degree of intensity for what I am trying to say?

Am I leaving somewhere for the other person to stand?

After I finish speaking, can they genuinely answer me?

And finally:

Are the words already here?

If they are, perhaps say them.

The point is not to perform a ritual before every sentence.

The point is to develop enough sensitivity that these judgments increasingly become part of how we move.

A musician does not calculate every movement of the hand while performing.

A practiced conversationalist should not have to either.

The aspiration is not constant self-monitoring.

It is cultivated responsiveness.

Good Talk

The phrase good talk sounds almost trivial.

We say it after enjoyable conversations.

Sometimes sarcastically after terrible ones.

But the phrase contains a serious question.

What does it mean for talk to be good?

Certainly it should be truthful.

Often it should be clear.

Sometimes it should be brave.

Sometimes gentle.

Sometimes concise.

Sometimes exhaustive.

Sometimes immediate.

Sometimes delayed.

Sometimes good talk is a speech.

Sometimes it is three words.

Sometimes it is the joke that releases a room from tension.

Sometimes it is the question nobody else thought to ask.

Sometimes it is telling somebody something they do not want to hear.

Sometimes it is refusing to say what you have not yet earned the right to say.

And sometimes it is a text to your father telling him that you might not be in the city where he expects to find you a month from now.

The measure cannot therefore be maximum expression.

Nor can it be perfect control.

Human beings do not meet one another through completed scripts.

We speak.

We listen.

We misread.

We clarify.

We disclose.

We retract.

We joke.

We resist.

We apologize.

We ask again.

Conversation succeeds not necessarily when nothing remains to be said, but when what has been said allows something truthful to happen afterward.

Perhaps that is one modest telos of dialogue:

to make the next honest interaction possible.

And perhaps this is why timing matters so much.

A good sentence does not merely describe reality.

Spoken into a relationship at the right moment, it changes what reality can become next.

The challenge is not to speak as much as possible.

Nor to wait as long as possible.

It is to become attentive enough to recognize when truth, purpose, relationship, and occasion have converged sufficiently for speech.

Sometimes we know immediately.

Sometimes we do not.

And when we do not, the silence between the urge to speak and the availability of the words need not be emptiness.

It may be where the next possibility between us is taking form.
